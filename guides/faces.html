Why faces look wide on ultrawide monitors

The math behind "fat face syndrome" — and the non-linear fix.

You bought an ultrawide monitor to see more. Then you play a 16:9 video and either get black bars on the sides, or — if you stretch — everyone looks like they gained 30 pounds. Here's why, and what actually works.

The problem: uniform stretching

A 16:9 video is, say, 1920×1080. Your 21:9 monitor is 3440×1440. To fill the screen, you need to stretch the width by 3440/1920 = 1.79× while the height only stretches by 1440/1080 = 1.33×. The result: every pixel becomes 1.79/1.33 = 1.34× wider than tall. A round face becomes an oval — 34% too wide.

This is called anamorphic distortion — the same effect you get watching a CinemaScope movie on a square TV. It's not a bug in your monitor or player; it's basic geometry.

Why you can't just "unstretch" the center

What if you stretch the edges more than the center? Then faces in the middle stay close to their natural proportions, while the sides — backgrounds, landscapes, crowd scenes — absorb the extra width. This is exactly what Smoothie does.

The key insight: viewers look at the center of the frame. That's where faces, dialogue, and action live. The edges are peripheral — a stretch there is barely noticed. So Smoothie applies a logarithmic stretch curve:

stretch(x) = 1.0 at center → up to 1.86× at edges

At the exact center of the frame, the stretch factor is exactly 1.0× — zero distortion. As you move toward the edges, stretching increases gradually. A face at center-left or center-right gets maybe 1.05× wider — imperceptible. A background tree at the far edge gets 1.86× wider — invisible because you're not looking there.

The result

With Smoothie:

✅ The full frame fills your ultrawide screen — no black bars
✅ Faces stay true (center stretch = 1.0×)
✅ Nothing is cropped or lost
✅ Works on Netflix and other DRM streams
✅ No visible seams or artifacts

Why hasn't anyone done this before?

Non-linear video warping requires real-time pixel manipulation, which is expensive. Historically this needed WebGL canvas overlays — which break on DRM-protected content. Ultrawider uses an SVG displacement map filter that the browser's compositor applies directly to the video element, making it work everywhere — including Netflix — with near-zero CPU cost.
