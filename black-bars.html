How to Remove Black Bars on an Ultrawide Monitor

The 2026 guide to making 16:9 video fill a 21:9 or 32:9 screen — four real methods, with the honest trade-offs of each.

Short answer: black bars exist because nearly all video is produced in 16:9, and your monitor isn't. You can crop the video, stretch it, or warp it non-linearly. Cropping loses the edges, linear stretching makes faces ~30% wider, and non-linear warping keeps the center of the image untouched while stretching only the edges — which is the only method where people on screen still look like themselves.

Why you get black bars in the first place

A 21:9 monitor is about 31% wider than the 16:9 frame almost every YouTube video, stream, and broadcast is delivered in. A 32:9 super ultrawide is 100% wider. When a 16:9 video plays fullscreen, your display fills the missing width with black pillars — on a 34" ultrawide that's roughly a quarter of the panel doing nothing; on a 49" it's half.

Games solve this natively: they just render more world at the sides. Video can't — the pixels beyond 16:9 were never filmed. So every solution is some way of inventing that missing width. The four methods below differ only in where they hide the damage.

Method 1: Zoom / crop (built into some players)

Scale the video until it fills the width, cut off the top and bottom

Some video players (VLC, MPC-HC, some TV apps) have a "crop to fill" or zoom mode. The image fills the screen and geometry stays perfectly correct — you just see less of it.

  • Zero distortion — circles stay circles
  • Works on any content, including DRM-protected sites
  • You lose ~25% of the image on 21:9 (top and bottom)
  • Subtitles, HUDs and lower-thirds often get cut off
  • Barely available in browsers, where most video actually lives

Method 2: GPU display scaling

Force the whole display to stretch via NVIDIA/AMD drivers

NVIDIA Control Panel → "Adjust desktop size and position" → Full-screen scaling (AMD: GPU Scaling → Full Panel). This stretches everything, not just the video.

  • System-wide, works in any app
  • Linear stretch: everything becomes ~30% wider, including faces, text and your desktop
  • Meant for lower-than-native game resolutions, not for watching video — you have to toggle it manually every time

Method 3: Linear-stretch browser extensions

Stretch the video element with CSS

The classic ultrawide extensions apply a uniform horizontal scale to the video. Simple, reliable — and the reason "everyone looks fat" is the most common complaint about watching stretched video.

  • One click, works on most sites
  • No pixel access needed, so DRM sites work too
  • Uniform distortion: a face in the center gets exactly as stretched as the edges — people look ~30% wider
  • Your brain never fully tunes it out, especially on close-ups and news anchors

Method 4: Non-linear warp (what we build)

Keep the center of the frame untouched, stretch only the edges

Old TVs called a crude version of this "smart stretch". The modern version runs a warp curve over the video in a WebGL shader: the central ~60% of the frame maps 1:1 — mathematically zero distortion where faces, players and action live — while the outer edges absorb all of the stretch, where peripheral vision barely registers it.

That's what Ultrawider does. It detects your exact aspect ratio (21:9, 32:9, anything), computes the right curve, and applies it the moment a video goes fullscreen. When a site blocks pixel access (DRM), it falls back to a clean crop automatically.

  • Faces and center content stay geometrically correct
  • Fills the entire panel — no bars, no cropped subtitles
  • Works per-site with remembered preferences
  • Needs pixel access, so pure-DRM sites (Netflix-class) get crop mode instead
  • The outer edges are technically distorted — you're choosing where the distortion goes, not whether it exists

FAQ

Why is all video still 16:9 when ultrawide monitors exist?
Because 16:9 is the production and delivery standard — cameras, editing pipelines, YouTube's encoder and TV broadcasts all target it. Ultrawide owners are a fast-growing but still small share of viewers, so nobody masters content for 21:9. That's unlikely to change soon; the fix has to happen on your side of the screen.
Does stretching video hurt quality?
Cropping discards pixels; stretching (linear or non-linear) resamples them. On a good scaler the sharpness cost is negligible compared to compression artifacts already in the stream. The visible difference between methods is geometry, not sharpness.
Why do some sites only offer crop mode?
Sites with DRM (Widevine and friends) block reading video pixels entirely, and a warp shader needs to read them. Cropping is pure CSS scaling, so it works everywhere.
Does this work on 32:9 super ultrawides?
Yes — the warp curve is computed from your actual aspect ratio. On 32:9 the edges absorb more stretch, but the center stays 1:1 just the same. Crop mode on 32:9 costs too much image to be practical, which makes the warp approach even more relevant there.
